\chapter{Results and Discussion}\label{ch:results}

\section{Results and Analysis}\label{sec:results}
This section reports the comparison described in \cref{sec:validation}: OpenTrading 0.5.1 against OpenCode and DSH, on 300 tasks with the same model and data.
Accuracy comes first, then speed and cost, reliability, and an analysis of the errors.

\subsection{Accuracy}\label{sec:accuracy}

On compute, lookup and robustness tasks the three harnesses were within three tasks of each other, so plain calculation and retrieval did not separate them.
The difference came from memory: OpenTrading applied the rules that a user had given in an earlier session, including rules later updated or withdrawn, in 58 of 59 tasks, against 50 for DSH and 30 for OpenCode.
On the investor tasks all three were weak, near one half.

Compared on the same tasks with the sign test of \cref{eq:sign-test}, OpenTrading was ahead of OpenCode on 40 tasks and behind on 6 ($p=3.1\times10^{-7}$), and ahead of DSH on 19 and behind on 6 ($p=0.015$).
Both differences are unlikely if the harnesses were equally good.
The difference with DSH depends on the corrections of the grading described in \cref{sec:bench-scoring}: on the 181 tasks that the judge graded alone, OpenTrading was ahead of DSH on 19 tasks and behind on 12 before them ($p=0.28$), and ahead on 18 and behind on 5 after them ($p=0.011$).

\subsection{Speed and Cost}\label{sec:speed-cost}
\Cref{tab:speed} reports how long a trial took and how much work and money it needed.
A typical trial took under a minute in every harness: the median was 36 seconds for OpenTrading, 47 for OpenCode and 31 for DSH.
The harnesses differed in their slow trials.
The slowest tenth of OpenTrading's trials took more than 97 seconds, against 228 for DSH and 300 for OpenCode, and only one OpenTrading trial took more than five minutes, against 17 for DSH and 30 for OpenCode.
OpenTrading also needed fewer model requests, 3.8 per trial against about 7 for the others, and its cost per correct answer was less than half that of OpenCode and about half that of DSH.

\subsection{Reliability}\label{sec:reliability}
OpenTrading returned an answer in a valid format in 299 of 300 trials and never refused a task that had an answer (\cref{tab:reliability}).
OpenCode refused ten answerable tasks, all in the memory group, and gave 15 numbers outside the tolerance, against one for OpenTrading.
No harness gave a number on a task whose correct answer is a refusal, so none of them invented data on these 11 tasks.

\subsection{Error Analysis}\label{sec:error-analysis}
OpenTrading failed 21 tasks, DSH 34 and OpenCode 55.
The errors of OpenTrading and of the other two harnesses lie in different places, and they have different causes.

Seventeen of OpenTrading's 21 errors were investor tasks, which test a judgement about the future rather than the use of data.
The nine wrong buy, hold or sell calls went in both directions: four buys and two holds on shares that fell, and three sells on shares that rose.
OpenTrading therefore did not lean towards buying or selling; it chose wrongly company by company.
For example, it recommended buying PV Gas (GAS) on 30 October 2026, and the price fell from \num{78.0} to \num{69.3} thousand đồng by 23 November, a loss of 11.2\%.
No harness found the stock that rose most in the following weeks (0 of 4 for all); with six candidates per task and only four tasks, this says little.
All three were below the 11 of 16 calls of a rule that always answers \enquote{sell} in this falling market.
These tasks show the limit of a model reading past data, not a fault that a harness rule could fix.

Only four errors of OpenTrading lay outside the investor group.
In one compute task it named the wrong company as the one that reduced its leverage most, although its twelve numbers were all correct.
In one screening task it removed a company with a negative price-to-earnings ratio that the question still counted.
In one lookup it reported a change of $-0.61$ instead of $+0.61$, and the tolerance check rejected it.
In one memory task it ended without a final answer.
%% Evidence: E39, E46.

Memory tasks give the user's rules in an earlier session, such as \enquote{only shares with a price-to-earnings ratio below 15}, and later update or withdraw them.
OpenCode failed 29 of the 59 tasks, in four ways (\cref{tab:memory-errors}).
Most often it applied a rule wrongly, kept a rule that had been replaced, or ignored a rule entirely; nearly as often it refused, saying that it could not find the user's rules.
In one task the user had replaced the criteria with a price-to-book ratio below 3 and a price-to-earnings ratio below 35; OpenCode kept the old criteria and returned one company, PNJ, instead of the four that met the new ones (KDC, MSN, PNJ and SBT), while OpenTrading answered correctly.
DSH failed 9 memory tasks, six of them without an answer.
OpenTrading, by contrast, failed one memory task and needed only a few requests for the others, which explains both its accuracy and its speed on this group.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
RQ3 (\cref{sec:rq}) asked whether a harness designed for financial analysis answers better than a general-purpose harness when the model and the data are the same.
The results answer yes, but only for some kinds of question, and this section explains where the difference comes from and how it relates to earlier work.

\subsection{Answers to the Research Questions}\label{sec:rq-answers}
RQ1 asked which parts a harness must add around a model so that its analysis stays grounded, follows the investor's constraints and keeps actions under the user's control.
The answer of this project is the set of rules in \cref{sec:algorithm}: the stop gate and the grounding rules, figure tracing with arithmetic in the shell, tool search with a check of tool inputs, memory that only the user can change, the permission gate, and sub-agents for long tasks.
The automated tests show that each rule does what it was designed to do (\cref{sec:testing}).
The benchmark measured only part of this answer.
OpenTrading had one answer rejected for a number outside the tolerance, gave no number on any of the 11 tasks whose data does not exist, and ended one trial without an answer (\cref{tab:reliability}).
Tool search, the permission gate, skills and sub-agents were not used in the benchmark, so for them the answer rests on the design and the tests alone.
%% Evidence: E11, E45.

RQ2 asked how a data server should return its results so that the agent knows the unit, the source and the availability of each figure.
The data server returns every result in one envelope that states whether the data is available, its source and time, and, when data is missing, the reason and whether a retry may help (\cref{sec:tool-exposure}).
Units are stated for the price tools whose units have been checked, not yet for every tool.
A contract check calls the tools with live data and checks the envelope, but it is not part of the automated checks of each change.
The answer to RQ2 is therefore a working design whose two gaps are the coverage of units and a contract check that does not yet run automatically.
%% Evidence: E13.

RQ3 asked whether OpenTrading answers more financial tasks correctly than two general-purpose harnesses with the same model, data and scoring, and at what cost.
On the 300 tasks of the benchmark, OpenTrading answered 279 correctly (\qty{93.0}{\percent}), against 266 for DSH (\qty{88.7}{\percent}) and 245 for OpenCode (\qty{81.7}{\percent}), and both differences were unlikely under equal performance (\cref{sec:accuracy}).
Its cost per correct answer was about half that of DSH and less than half that of OpenCode, and its median time per trial lay between the two (\cref{sec:speed-cost}).
The difference came mostly from the memory tasks, and the limits of \cref{sec:threats} apply to it.

\subsection{Where the Harness Makes a Difference}\label{sec:harness-difference}
On 205 of the 300 tasks, the compute, lookup and robustness groups, the three harnesses were within three tasks of each other.
Once every harness had the same data tools and a shell for arithmetic, fetching a figure and computing with it did not depend on the harness: the model and the data did the work.

The difference came from the memory group, where OpenTrading answered 58 of 59 tasks against 50 for DSH and 30 for OpenCode.
These tasks depend on what the user said in an earlier session, which a model cannot recover from the data.
In OpenTrading memory is part of the harness: at the start of every turn the user's constraints are placed in the system part of the prompt (\cref{sec:memory,sec:memory-flow}), so the model does not need to search for them.
This group therefore favours OpenTrading by design: the benchmark's controller saved each rule through the same path as the user's form, in the user's exact words, while OpenCode could only resume the earlier session and DSH could resume it and search past sessions.
The other harnesses had to find the rules in the earlier session; they spent 15 to 20 requests on a typical memory task, against 4 for OpenTrading, and still often kept an outdated rule, gave no answer or, in the case of OpenCode, refused because they found none.
%% Evidence: E65.
The same difference explains most of OpenTrading's lead in speed and cost: fewer requests meant a shorter wait in the slowest trials and about half the cost per correct answer.
%% Evidence: E45, E46.

The investor group shows the opposite limit.
No harness beat a rule that always answers \enquote{sell}, and none found the stock that rose most in the four tasks that asked for it.
A harness can make sure that an answer rests on real data, but it cannot give the model foresight.
This supports the scope of the project (\cref{sec:scope}): OpenTrading aims at answers whose figures can be checked, not at predictions of prices.

\subsection{Relation to Earlier Work}\label{sec:related-results}
Research harnesses for software tasks showed that the interface between the model and its tools changes how well an agent performs \parencite{yang2024sweagent}.
The present results agree, and add two points from a controlled comparison in finance: the effect is concentrated in tasks that need state across sessions, and on tasks of plain retrieval and calculation it disappears.
That calculation did not separate the harnesses is consistent with work showing that arithmetic written as a program is more reliable than arithmetic in words \parencite{gao2023pal,chen2023program}, since all three harnesses calculated through a shell.
Long-term memory has been proposed as a function of the system around the model rather than of the model itself \parencite{packer2023memgpt}; the memory group gives measured support for this view in a financial setting.

Earlier studies found that language models invent financial figures \parencite{kang2023deficiency}, and that a strong model with retrieval answered wrongly or refused most questions on company filings \parencite{islam2023financebench}.
Here no harness gave a number on any task whose data does not exist, and OpenTrading had one answer rejected for a wrong number in 300.
The settings differ and the refusal tasks are few (11), so the numbers cannot be compared directly, but the result suggests that structured data tools leave less room for invented figures.
Finally, \textcite{kapoor2024agents} argued that evaluations of agents should control for cost and report it alongside accuracy; this benchmark reports both, and the cost per correct answer differed by about a factor of two, more in relative terms than accuracy did.


\subsection{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:threats}
Six limits apply to these results:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Every task ran once in each harness, so the results do not show how stable the answers are across runs.
  \item The memory tasks, where most of the difference lies, were written by the group that built OpenTrading's memory, and their form may suit its design.
  \item The investor tasks cover one falling market over three weeks, so they are a signal about this period, not about investment skill.
  \item The judge was checked only on clearly correct and clearly wrong answers.
  \item For 119 tasks, the answers of OpenCode and DSH come from an earlier run with the same model and data but older versions of these harnesses; most of these tasks are lookups on which the three harnesses scored almost the same.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Recommendations}\label{sec:recommendations}
The results lead to five recommendations for the harness and its evaluation.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Turn figure tracing into a check. In version 0.5.1 it records figures that match no tool result but blocks an answer only in turns without tools (\cref{sec:figure-tracing}); the records of real use can show how often it would block a correct answer before it is enforced.
  \item Measure each rule on its own. The benchmark ran every task once and did not use tool search, the permission gate, skills or sub-agents, so repeated runs and runs with one rule switched off at a time are needed to show what each rule adds.
  \item Test memory with tasks written outside the group. The memory tasks, where the harnesses differed most, were written by the group that built the memory of OpenTrading (\cref{sec:threats}).
  \item Keep buy, hold and sell calls in their place. No harness did better than a rule that always answers \enquote{sell} in this period (\cref{sec:error-analysis}), so a call should be shown as an analysis of the data with its risks, as the system prompt already requires, not as a forecast.
  \item Run the contract check of the data server with every change, and extend the stated units from the checked price tools to every tool that returns an amount (\cref{sec:rq-answers}).
\end{enumerate}
For users, the practical advice follows from the same results: check each figure against the source that the answer names, and treat a call as one input to a decision that remains theirs.

\section{Personal Reflection (Optional)}\label{sec:reflection}
The group draws four lessons from the project.

First, the choice of language should be made with the first version of the agent loop.
The harness began as a Python scaffold, and the group chose TypeScript in week 3 (\cref{sec:stack}); the rewrite was early enough to be cheap, but choosing the language together with the terminal library from the start would have avoided it.

Second, a rule should be measured before and after it changes.
Measurements in week 10 showed weaknesses in the system prompt and in the calculation tools, and version 0.5.0 replaced the calculation tools with arithmetic in the shell (\cref{sec:stages}).
From week 11 the group asked for a small paired measurement with each change to a prompt or a tool (\cref{sec:quality}), and earlier changes would have been easier to judge with the same rule.

Third, tests and the benchmark answer different questions.
The tests show that a rule behaves as designed, while only the benchmark shows whether the answers improve, and even then only for the rules that it uses (\cref{sec:rq-answers}).
Keeping the two apart made the claims of this report smaller but easier to defend.

Fourth, data sources fail more often than expected.
A release of the data library was switched off during the project (\cref{sec:risk}), and reporting a failure as a failure, rather than as empty data, turned out to be part of the design rather than an extra.
In the same way, tracking work as issues only from week 10 left the early decisions spread across pull requests; tracking them from the start would have made the history of the project easier to follow.
